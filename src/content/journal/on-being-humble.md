I grew up in a family where being humble was considered a virtue. We were taught to respect others, not to brag about our achievements, and never to think that we were better than anyone else. It was simply how I was raised, and for a long time, I assumed that most people shared these values.

When I moved to Japan to pursue my PhD, this belief became even stronger. I admired the modesty I encountered in everyday life, the respect people showed one another, and the way they could be highly accomplished without constantly needing to demonstrate it. Of course, Japan has its own problems, but this particular aspect of Japanese culture felt very familiar to me. It reminded me of the values I had grown up with.

Then I returned to Bosnia and Herzegovina.

I quickly realized that being humble was not always an advantage. Quite the opposite, actually. I often felt that modesty was mistaken for weakness, politeness for insecurity, and being quiet for having nothing important to say.

I had to learn to be more aggressive. To speak louder, insist on things, defend myself, and sometimes fight for things that I thought should have been perfectly normal.

And I hated it.

Not because I was afraid of confrontation, but because I didn't like the person I sometimes had to become. It felt as though I had spent years learning one way of behaving, only to return home and discover that I needed to do almost the opposite to get through everyday life.

I sometimes think this is one of the problems we have in the Balkans. We seem to reward loudness more than competence, aggression more than kindness, and self-promotion more than actual achievement. Perhaps this is also one of the reasons why progress can be so difficult.

Of course, this is a huge generalization. There are wonderful, humble people everywhere, including Bosnia and Herzegovina. I have been fortunate to know many of them. I am only describing what I experienced and how it affected me.

> **A NOTE ON HUMILITY**
>
> **Being humble**  
> Recognizing your own abilities and achievements without feeling superior to others.
>
> **Humility**  
> The quality behind that behavior: understanding that no matter how much you know or accomplish, you still have things to learn, and everyone deserves respect.
>
> *Neither means thinking less of yourself or allowing others to treat you poorly.*

There is another lesson here about traveling. We often talk about how wonderful it is to explore the world, experience different cultures, and broaden our horizons. And it really is. But we rarely mention that traveling can also make it harder to return home. Once you have experienced different ways of living and treating people, some things become difficult to accept again.

Sometimes traveling changes you in ways that are not entirely comfortable.

Over the years, I have learned to adapt. I know when to stand up for myself, when to be more direct, and when being polite simply isn't enough. But I haven't stopped believing in humility. I still try to practice it every day, even when the environment around me doesn't particularly encourage it.

I have also learned not to expect everyone to value the same things I do.

Perhaps the most useful lesson from all of this is about choosing the people around us. Pay attention to how people speak, how they behave, and especially how they treat others. Notice whether they listen, whether they can admit they are wrong, and how they behave toward people who have less power or influence than they do.

I have also learned to pay attention to how I feel around people. When someone's words or actions resonate with me, I try to recognize why. Often, it is because I see in them the same values I hold myself. These are usually my kind of people, the ones with whom conversations feel natural and relationships don't require too much effort.

And it works the other way around, too. When someone's behavior or values don't sit well with me, I have learned to notice that feeling, quietly acknowledge it, and move on. There is no need to judge them, argue with them, or try to change them. We simply don't have to be close.

Most importantly, I have learned never to force a connection just to feel accepted. Not everyone needs to like us, and we don't need to belong everywhere.

Look for humble people. They are often the ones worth keeping close.

And when you find yourself in an environment where humility is not appreciated, recognize it for what it is. Adjust when you need to, protect yourself, but don't confuse that environment's expectations with what makes someone a good person.

I still believe humility is a virtue. I just no longer believe that being humble means allowing others to walk over you.
