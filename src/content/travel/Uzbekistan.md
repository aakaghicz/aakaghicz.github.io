Uzbekistan had been on my travel list for quite some time, particularly
because of Samarkand and its extraordinary Silk Road history. I finally
had an opportunity to make the trip and spent **five days in
Uzbekistan**, visiting **Tashkent, Bukhara, and Samarkand**.

Five days is not a lot of time for a country with so much history, but
good transport connections between the major cities make this route
surprisingly manageable.

This post is a short overview of the journey. I will write separately
about Tashkent, Bukhara, and Samarkand, with more photographs and
details from each city.

## Why Uzbekistan

The idea of visiting Uzbekistan actually started with my uncle. He travelled there 46 years ago, and over the years I heard him talk about that journey so many times that, at some point, I simply wanted to see the place for myself. Interestingly, whenever I mentioned that I was going to Uzbekistan, I often got the same reaction: _Why Uzbekistan? What is there to see?_ It made me realize how little many of us know about places that fall outside the usual travel routes.

In a way, this trip also motivated me to start writing about my travels. We often look at the world through a surprisingly narrow window, shaped by the places, cultures and histories we already know. Travelling gives us a chance to make that window a little wider. You meet people whose everyday lives are different from your own, discover histories you barely knew existed, and experience cultures that you might otherwise only encounter as a name on a map. I think that has the capacity to open your mind in a very natural way. The world becomes bigger, more complex, and somehow more interesting - and you begin to appreciate it with a little more curiosity and a lot fewer assumptions.

## History of Uzbekistan

To understand Uzbekistan, even briefly, it helps to realize just how old the history of this region is. For centuries, the territory of modern Uzbekistan stood at the crossroads between **China, Persia, India, the Middle East and Europe**, and cities such as Samarkand and Bukhara became important centres along the Silk Road. The region was influenced or ruled at different times by the Achaemenid Persian Empire, Alexander the Great and his successors, the Kushans, Turkic states and, from the eighth century onward, expanding Islamic dynasties. The Mongol conquest under Genghis Khan in the early thirteenth century devastated many of its cities, but another extraordinary period followed in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries under **Amir Timur (Tamerlane) and the Timurids**. Samarkand became the centre of Timur's empire, while his grandson **Ulugh Beg** turned it into an important centre of science and astronomy. In later centuries the region was divided among states including the Emirate of Bukhara and the khanates of Khiva and Kokand, before much of it came under Russian imperial control in the nineteenth century. Uzbekistan later became part of the Soviet Union as the Uzbek SSR and finally declared independence in **1991**. Once you know even this very short history, the architecture of Bukhara and Samarkand begins to feel less like a collection of beautiful monuments and more like visible layers of more than two thousand years of cultural exchange.

## Important facts

Uzbekistan is the most populous country in Central Asia, with a population of around **39.5 million people in 2026**. Tashkent is by far the largest city, with a little over **3.2 million inhabitants**, while Samarkand has around **600,000**. Bukhara is considerably smaller, which is something you immediately feel when walking through its historic centre. The names themselves also carry traces of the region's long history. **Uzbekistan** simply means “land of the Uzbeks,” with the Persian suffix _-stan_ meaning “land” or “place.” **Tashkent** is usually understood from the Turkic _tash_ (stone) and _kent_ (city), hence “Stone City,” although the city was historically also known as Chach or Shash. The origins of **Bukhara** are less certain: one historical interpretation connects it with the Sogdian _βuxārak_, meaning something like “fortunate place,” while another links it to _vihāra_, the Sanskrit term for a Buddhist monastery. **Samarkand** is even harder to translate with certainty. The ending _-kand/-kent_ means “town” or “city,” but historians have proposed several explanations for the first part of the name. What I found particularly interesting is that even the names of these cities reveal how many languages, peoples and cultures have passed through this part of Central Asia.

## The Route

I followed a fairly simple route through three of Uzbekistan's
best-known cities:

**Tashkent → Bukhara → Samarkand → Tashkent**

I started in **Tashkent**, where I spent my first day exploring the
capital. From there, I took a short domestic flight to **Bukhara**,
which takes a little over an hour. I spent one day and one night in
Bukhara before taking an early morning train to **Samarkand**.

The train journey from Bukhara to Samarkand took a little over two
hours. I then spent two days in Samarkand before returning to Tashkent
by train for my final day in Uzbekistan.

### My five-day itinerary

| Day | Where | Travel |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Tashkent | Exploring the city |
| Day 2 | Tashkent → Bukhara | Around 1 hour by plane |
| Day 3 | Bukhara → Samarkand | Around 2 hours by train |
| Day 4 | Samarkand | Full day in the city |
| Day 5 | Samarkand → Tashkent | Morning train to Tashkent |


This turned out to be a very practical route for a short first visit.
The distances between the three cities are significant, but the
combination of domestic flights and trains makes it possible to see all
three without spending too much of the trip in transit.

## Getting Around Uzbekistan

There are several ways to travel between Uzbekistan's major cities,
including domestic flights, trains and cars.

For this trip, I used a combination of **air travel and trains**. Flying
between Tashkent and Bukhara saved time, while the train was a
convenient option for travelling from Bukhara to Samarkand and from
Samarkand back to Tashkent.

For longer distances within the cities, I used **Yandex Go**, which made
getting around very straightforward. In the historic centres of Bukhara
and Samarkand, however, walking was often the best option. Many of the
main sights are relatively close to one another, and walking is also a
much better way to experience the streets, markets and architecture.

## Tashkent

Tashkent was both the beginning and the end of my journey. It is very
different from Bukhara and Samarkand: larger, more modern, and with a
mixture of Soviet, Islamic and contemporary architecture.

Among the main places on my Tashkent itinerary were:

-   Hazrati Imam (Khast Imam) Complex
-   Chorsu Bazaar
-   Tashkent Metro and its decorated stations
-   Kosmonavtlar Metro Station
-   Amir Temur Square
-   State Museum of the Temurids
-   central Tashkent and the area around Alisher Navoi Theatre

The Tashkent Metro deserves particular mention. It is not simply a means
of transportation; many stations are architectural spaces in their own
right.

I will write more about Tashkent in a separate post.

## Bukhara

Bukhara was the shortest stop of the trip: one day and one night.
Fortunately, its historic centre is compact and many of its most
important monuments can be explored on foot.

My Bukhara itinerary included:

-   Lyabi-Hauz
-   Nadir Divan-Begi Madrasa
-   the old trading domes
-   Poi Kalyan Complex
-   Kalyan Minaret
-   Kalyan Mosque
-   Mir-i-Arab Madrasa
-   Ark Fortress
-   Bolo Hauz Mosque
-   the streets and bazaars of the old city

Bukhara has a very different atmosphere from Tashkent. Rather than
moving between isolated monuments, much of the experience comes from
simply walking through the historic centre.

## Samarkand

Samarkand was the city I was most looking forward to visiting, and I
deliberately allocated two days to it.

The main places on my Samarkand itinerary were:

-   Registan
-   Ulugh Beg Madrasa
-   Sher-Dor Madrasa
-   Tilla-Kari Madrasa and Mosque
-   Gur-e-Amir Mausoleum
-   Bibi-Khanym Mosque
-   Siyob Bazaar
-   Shah-i-Zinda
-   Ulugh Beg Observatory
-   Afrosiyob Museum

Registan naturally dominates any first visit to Samarkand, but the city
has much more to offer. Shah-i-Zinda, Gur-e-Amir, Bibi-Khanym and the
story of Ulugh Beg add very different layers to the history of the city.

I ended up returning to Registan several times, at different times of
the day. The architecture changes noticeably with the light, making
morning, afternoon and evening visits feel surprisingly different.

## Five Days --- Is It Enough?

Five days is certainly not enough to see Uzbekistan in depth, but it is
enough for a very good introduction.

The combination of **Tashkent, Bukhara and Samarkand** works
particularly well for a first visit. Tashkent provides an introduction
to modern Uzbekistan, while Bukhara and Samarkand take you much further
into the history of Central Asia and the Silk Road.

This time, the key was not to try to see everything. With limited time, I found it
more useful to concentrate on these three cities and leave the rest of
Uzbekistan for another journey.

If I were to do this trip again, I would definitely add **Khiva and the Fergana Valley** to the itinerary. That would, of course, require more time. I packed as much as I reasonably could into the five days I had, and for a first visit, Tashkent, Bukhara and Samarkand gave me a wonderful introduction to the country. But Uzbekistan is not a place I would rush through a second time. Next time, I would give myself **at least ten days** - enough time to travel more slowly, go beyond the most famous cities, and leave some space for unexpected discoveries. There is simply too much history, culture and everyday life here to absorb in a few days. I left with the feeling that I had seen a lot, but also that there was still so much more to understand. And perhaps that is one of the best reasons to return.
